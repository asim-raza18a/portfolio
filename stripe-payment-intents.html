Section 1Who this is for

This guide is for an engineer who has been asked to build a payment flow and has decided, or been told, to build it with the Payment Intents API rather than a prebuilt checkout page.

It assumes you are comfortable with REST APIs and HTTP. It does not assume you know anything about payments. Where a concept is specific to the card industry, such as authorisation versus capture, it is explained at the point you need it.

The guide is written to be read in order the first time and used as a reference afterwards. Sections 1 to 8 are the path to a working payment. Sections 9 to 16 cover the parts that are easy to skip and expensive to skip. Sections 17 and 18 cover testing and launch.

What this guide covers that a quickstart does not

A quickstart gets you a successful test payment. That is useful, and it is roughly fifteen minutes of work. The gap between a successful test payment and a payment system you can run a business on is where most of the engineering effort actually goes. That gap is the subject of this guide.

Specifically, it covers what happens when things do not go to plan. A customer closes the tab halfway through authentication. Your server times out after the charge succeeded but before you recorded it. A webhook arrives twice, or out of order, or six hours late. A card is declined for a reason you can retry and another that you must not. These are not edge cases. At any meaningful volume they happen every day, and the quality of your integration is determined almost entirely by how it handles them.

What you need before you start

  • A Stripe account with access to a test environment. Test mode keys are prefixed sk_test_ and live keys sk_live_.
  • A server you can run. The Payment Intents API requires a server. Your secret key must never reach the browser.
  • An HTTPS endpoint reachable from the internet for webhooks, or the Stripe CLI for local forwarding during development.
  • A decision about which payment methods you intend to accept, because it affects how you configure the integration. Section 2 covers this.

Section 2Choosing your integration before you write anything

Before building with Payment Intents directly, be sure it is the right choice. For a large share of integrations it is not, and the wrong choice here costs weeks that are hard to recover.

Stripe offers several ways to take a payment, and they differ mainly in how much of the flow you own. Stripe's own current guidance recommends the Checkout Sessions API with the Payment Element for most integrations, rather than driving Payment Intents yourself. That recommendation is worth taking seriously, and this guide says so even though its subject is the harder path.

The three broad options

ApproachYou ownStripe ownsTypical effort
Payment LinksA URLEverything else, including the pageMinutes
Checkout Sessions with the Payment ElementYour product and pricing logic, your success pageThe payment form, authentication flow, payment method rendering, localisation, and compliance with new network rules as they arriveDays
Payment Intents directlyAll of the above, plus the state machineThe payment railsWeeks

When driving Payment Intents yourself is justified

  • The payment step is embedded in a flow you fully control and a redirect or hosted component is unacceptable for product reasons. Note that the Payment Element can be embedded, so "we want it on our own page" alone is not sufficient justification.
  • You need to authorise now and capture later with your own business logic deciding when and how much to capture. Section 9 covers this.
  • Your checkout is not a checkout. Payments triggered by an event in your system rather than by a customer clicking a button, such as usage settlement or scheduled billing, do not fit a hosted page.
  • You are building a platform where the payment flow differs per merchant and must be configured programmatically.

When it is not justified

If your requirement is a checkout page that takes a card and charges it, you are very likely to spend several weeks reimplementing a solved problem, then spend more weeks maintaining it as payment method support and regulatory requirements change. The hosted components are updated for you when card network rules change. Your bespoke flow is not.

A useful test. Write down the specific product requirement that the Payment Element cannot satisfy. If you cannot name one in a sentence, use the Payment Element. If you can, continue with this guide, and note the requirement somewhere, because in eighteen months someone will ask why the payment flow is bespoke and nobody will remember.

The rest of this guide assumes you have made that decision deliberately.

Section 3Core concepts

Four objects and one secret account for most of what you need to understand. Getting these clear now saves a great deal of confusion later, because the API names are similar and the distinctions matter.

The PaymentIntent

A PaymentIntent represents your intention to collect a specific amount from a specific customer. It is created before you know whether the payment will succeed, and it persists through every attempt. If a card is declined and the customer tries another, that is the same PaymentIntent with a second attempt against it, not a new one.

This is the single most important idea in the API. Older payment APIs, including Stripe's own earlier Charges API, modelled a payment as a single action that either worked or did not. Modern card payments are not like that. They can require a step from the customer partway through, they can complete minutes or hours later, and they can be attempted several times. The PaymentIntent exists to hold that state.

A PaymentIntent creates at most one successful charge. That is a guarantee worth internalising, because it is what makes the object safe to retry against.

The PaymentMethod

A PaymentMethod represents the instrument being used, such as a specific card or a bank account. It is a separate object from the PaymentIntent. You attach a PaymentMethod to a PaymentIntent in order to attempt payment with it.

PaymentMethods can be created on the client, which is how card details avoid touching your server. They can also be saved to a Customer for later use, which Section 15 covers.

The Customer

A Customer object groups payment methods, payments and subscriptions under one record. It is optional for a one-off payment and necessary for anything involving saved cards or recurring billing.

Create the Customer before the first payment if you expect any repeat relationship. Associating payments retrospectively is possible but tedious, and the Customer record is where saved payment methods live.

The Charge

When a PaymentIntent succeeds it produces a Charge. The Charge holds the record of the actual money movement, including the balance transaction, the fee breakdown and the receipt. The PaymentIntent exposes the most recent one as latest_charge.

You rarely create Charges directly in a modern integration. You will read them when you need fee details, receipt URLs, or the full history of attempts against a payment. To see every attempt including the failures, list charges filtered by the payment_intent parameter rather than reading latest_charge.

The client secret

Every PaymentIntent has a client_secret. This string lets client-side code act on that one PaymentIntent, and only that one. You pass it to the browser so that Stripe.js can confirm the payment and handle any authentication step.

Handle the client secret carefully. It is not as dangerous as your secret key, but it is not public either. Anyone holding it can see the PaymentIntent's details and attempt to confirm it. Return it only to the authenticated customer the payment belongs to, send it over HTTPS, and do not log it, include it in URLs, or write it to analytics.

Section 4The status lifecycle

A PaymentIntent has seven possible statuses. Your integration is essentially a response to this state machine, so it is worth understanding all seven before writing code against any of them.

The seven statuses

StatusMeaningWho acts next
requires_payment_methodNo payment method is attached, or the last one failed. This is the starting state and the state a failed attempt returns to.The customer
requires_confirmationA payment method is attached but confirmation has not been requested. Only appears when you confirm manually.You or the customer
requires_actionAn extra step is needed, most often 3D Secure authentication, sometimes a redirect to a bank.The customer
processingStripe is processing the payment. Common with asynchronous methods such as direct debits, briefly possible with cards.Nobody. Wait.
requires_captureThe payment is authorised but funds are not yet taken. Only occurs when capture method is manual.You
succeededThe payment is complete and the funds are in your Stripe balance. Fulfil the order.You
canceledThe PaymentIntent is terminated and cannot be used again.Nobody

The transitions that matter

Failure returns to requires_payment_method. When a card is declined, the PaymentIntent does not move to a failed state. It returns to requires_payment_method so the customer can try a different card against the same intent. This is deliberate and it is why you should not treat a decline as the end of a payment. Your UI should offer another attempt rather than sending the customer back to the start of your checkout.

requires_action is not a failure. It is the most commonly mishandled status. An integration that treats anything other than succeeded as an error will reject a large share of legitimate European card payments, because authentication is mandatory for many of them. Section 8 covers this in full.

processing is terminal for your request but not for the payment. You will not learn the outcome synchronously. You learn it from a webhook, possibly days later for some payment methods. Any code that assumes it knows the result at the end of the HTTP request is wrong for these methods.

canceled is final. A canceled PaymentIntent cannot be revived. Create a new one. Stripe may also cancel a PaymentIntent automatically if it has been confirmed too many times, which is a protection against runaway retry loops in your code.

Writing code against the status

The single most robust pattern is to switch on status and handle every value explicitly, including the ones you do not expect.

switch (intent.status) {
  case 'succeeded':
    return fulfil(intent);
  case 'requires_action':
    return sendToClientForAuthentication(intent.client_secret);
  case 'requires_payment_method':
    return askForAnotherCard(intent.last_payment_error);
  case 'processing':
    return showPendingState(intent);
  case 'requires_capture':
    return holdForCapture(intent);
  case 'requires_confirmation':
    return confirmServerSide(intent);
  case 'canceled':
    return startOver();
  default:
    // Stripe may add statuses. Fail loudly rather than silently.
    logUnexpectedStatus(intent.status);
    return showGenericError();
}

The default branch matters. Payment APIs gain capabilities, and a switch that silently falls through on an unrecognised status will produce a customer who paid and an order that was never created.

Section 5Creating a PaymentIntent

Creation happens on your server, as early as you reasonably can. Stripe's guidance is to create the PaymentIntent as soon as you know the amount, so that every attempted payment is recorded against it.

The minimum request

const stripe = require('stripe')(process.env.STRIPE_SECRET_KEY);

const intent = await stripe.paymentIntents.create({
  amount: 2000,
  currency: 'gbp',
  automatic_payment_methods: { enabled: true },
});

Two parameters are required: amount and currency. Everything else is optional, and most of the optional parameters exist to answer questions you will have later.

Amount, and the mistake everyone makes once

Amounts are integers in the smallest unit of the currency. 2000 with currency: 'gbp' is twenty pounds. Sending 20.00 will either error or charge twenty pence, depending on how your code coerces it.

Keep money as integers throughout your own system too. Floating point arithmetic on currency produces rounding errors that surface during reconciliation, usually months later and usually at the worst time. If you have a decimal type available in your database, use it, and convert to minor units at the boundary.

Zero-decimal currencies are the exception to the general rule. In JPY, an amount of 2000 is two thousand yen, not twenty. If you handle multiple currencies, do not hardcode a divisor of 100 anywhere.

Parameters worth setting from the start

ParameterWhy
metadataYour own identifiers, such as order ID or internal customer ID. This is the single most useful optional parameter. Metadata appears in the Dashboard, in exports and on the objects delivered by webhooks, which makes reconciliation dramatically easier. Set it at creation; do not plan to add it later.
customerLinks the payment to a Customer. Required if you want to save the payment method, and useful for support staff looking at a customer's history.
descriptionAppears in the Dashboard and in some receipts. Write it for the human who will read it during a dispute, not for your logs.
statement_descriptor_suffixWhat the customer sees on their bank statement. An unrecognisable descriptor is a leading cause of chargebacks that are not actually fraud.
receipt_emailStripe sends a receipt in live mode. Useful if you are not sending your own.
capture_methodSet to manual if you need to authorise now and capture later. See Section 9. Changing this after creation is not possible, so decide at creation.
setup_future_usageSignals that you intend to reuse the payment method. Affects how Stripe handles regional authentication requirements. See Section 15.

A realistic creation call

const intent = await stripe.paymentIntents.create({
  amount: order.totalMinorUnits,
  currency: order.currency,
  customer: customer.stripeId,
  description: `Order ${order.reference}`,
  statement_descriptor_suffix: 'NORTHSIDE',
  automatic_payment_methods: { enabled: true },
  metadata: {
    order_id: order.id,
    order_reference: order.reference,
    environment: process.env.APP_ENV,
  },
}, {
  idempotencyKey: `pi-create-${order.id}`,
});

Note the idempotency key on the second argument. Section 10 explains why it is there and why the value is derived from your order rather than generated randomly at call time.

Note also environment in the metadata. When a staging deployment is accidentally pointed at live keys, which happens, that field is how you find the resulting payments quickly.

Automatic payment methods

Setting automatic_payment_methods: { enabled: true } lets Stripe decide which payment methods to offer based on the amount, the currency and the customer's location, using what you have enabled in your Dashboard settings. This is the recommended default. It means adding a new payment method is a configuration change rather than a deployment.

The alternative is to specify payment_method_types explicitly. Do this only when you have a specific reason, such as deliberately restricting to cards for a flow that cannot handle asynchronous settlement. An explicit list is a list you now have to maintain.

When to create it

Create the PaymentIntent when the amount is known and the customer is about to pay, not at the moment they click the final button. Creating it slightly early gives you a stable object to attach attempts to, and it means a slow creation call does not sit between the customer's click and your payment form.

Do not create one on every page load of your checkout. You will accumulate abandoned PaymentIntents. They are harmless but they make your Dashboard noisy and your conversion metrics misleading. If the customer returns to a checkout they abandoned, retrieve and update the existing PaymentIntent rather than creating another.

// Reuse an existing intent for this order if one is still usable
let intent;
if (order.paymentIntentId) {
  intent = await stripe.paymentIntents.retrieve(order.paymentIntentId);
  const reusable = ['requires_payment_method', 'requires_confirmation', 'requires_action'];
  if (!reusable.includes(intent.status)) {
    intent = null;
  } else if (intent.amount !== order.totalMinorUnits) {
    intent = await stripe.paymentIntents.update(intent.id, {
      amount: order.totalMinorUnits,
    });
  }
}
if (!intent) {
  intent = await stripe.paymentIntents.create({ /* as above */ });
  await order.update({ paymentIntentId: intent.id });
}

Updating the amount matters if your basket can change after the PaymentIntent exists. An intent created for one amount and confirmed against a changed basket is a reconciliation problem.

Section 6Collecting payment details

Card details must not touch your server. This is not merely good practice. Handling raw card data pulls you into a compliance scope that is expensive and slow to satisfy, and there is no reason to accept it.

Why the client collects the details

Stripe.js and the Elements components render the card fields inside an iframe served by Stripe. The customer types into Stripe's page, visually embedded in yours. The details go from the browser to Stripe directly. Your server never sees a card number, which keeps your PCI DSS obligations at the lightest tier.

If you proxy card details through your own server, even once, even in a prototype, your compliance scope changes. Teams do this accidentally by logging a request body. Be careful with request logging on any endpoint near the payment flow.

Mounting the Payment Element

const stripe = Stripe('pk_test_...');

const elements = stripe.elements({
  clientSecret,
  appearance: { theme: 'stripe' },
});

const paymentElement = elements.create('payment');
paymentElement.mount('#payment-element');

The Payment Element renders whichever payment methods apply to this PaymentIntent, in the customer's language, with the right validation rules for their region. This is a large amount of work you are not doing.

Submitting

form.addEventListener('submit', async (event) => {
  event.preventDefault();
  setSubmitting(true);

  const { error } = await stripe.confirmPayment({
    elements,
    confirmParams: {
      return_url: 'https://example.com/checkout/complete',
    },
  });

  // Execution only reaches here if confirmation failed immediately.
  // On success, or where authentication is needed, the customer is
  // redirected to return_url and this code does not run.
  if (error) {
    if (error.type === 'card_error' || error.type === 'validation_error') {
      showMessage(error.message);
    } else {
      showMessage('Something went wrong. Your card has not been charged.');
    }
    setSubmitting(false);
  }
});
The control flow here surprises people. When confirmation succeeds, confirmPayment does not return. The browser navigates to return_url. Any code you write after the call runs only in the failure case. Developers who put their success handling after the call find it never executes.

Handling the return

Stripe appends the PaymentIntent's client secret to your return URL as a query parameter. On that page, retrieve the intent and inspect its status.

const clientSecret = new URLSearchParams(window.location.search)
  .get('payment_intent_client_secret');

const { paymentIntent } = await stripe.retrievePaymentIntent(clientSecret);

switch (paymentIntent.status) {
  case 'succeeded':
    showMessage('Payment received. Thank you.');
    break;
  case 'processing':
    showMessage('Payment processing. We will email you when it completes.');
    break;
  case 'requires_payment_method':
    showMessage('That payment was not successful. Please try another card.');
    break;
  default:
    showMessage('Something went wrong.');
    break;
}
This page tells the customer what happened. It must not be what tells your system. The customer can close the tab before the redirect completes, lose connectivity, or simply never arrive. Fulfilment is driven by webhooks, covered in Section 13. Treat this page as a courtesy to the customer and nothing more.

A note on error messages

Stripe returns messages on card errors that are written for customers and localised. Use them directly rather than substituting your own text. They are more accurate about what happened than any generic message you would write, and they have been tested on real customers.

For every other error type, do not show the raw message. A message about an invalid parameter is a bug report for you, not information for a customer. Show something generic that makes clear whether the card was charged, and log the detail with enough context to debug it.

Section 7Confirmation flows

Confirmation is the step that tells Stripe to attempt the payment. Where that call is made, and when, is one of the few genuinely architectural decisions in this integration.

Three ways to confirm

FlowHow it worksUse when
Client-side confirmationThe browser calls stripe.confirmPayment() with the client secret. Your server creates the intent and does nothing else until the webhook arrives.Standard checkout. This is the default and the right choice for most integrations.
Server-side confirmationYour server calls confirm with a payment method ID that the client created.You need to run business logic between collecting details and charging, such as a final inventory check or fraud screen.
Create and confirm togetherPass confirm: true on creation.Charging a saved payment method with no customer present. See Section 15.

Client-side confirmation

This is what Section 6 showed. Your server creates the PaymentIntent, hands over the client secret, and the browser does the rest. Authentication is handled for you by Stripe.js, including the redirect dance that some payment methods require.

Prefer this. It involves the least code on your side and it handles the authentication cases correctly without you having to think about them.

Server-side confirmation

Sometimes you need to do something between the customer entering their card and the charge being attempted. A common case is a final stock check on a scarce item. In that case the client creates a PaymentMethod and sends you its ID, and your server confirms.

// Client
const { paymentMethod, error } = await stripe.createPaymentMethod({
  elements,
});
if (error) { showMessage(error.message); return; }
await fetch('/api/pay', {
  method: 'POST',
  headers: { 'Content-Type': 'application/json' },
  body: JSON.stringify({
    orderId: order.id,
    paymentMethodId: paymentMethod.id,
  }),
});
// Server
const stock = await reserveStock(order);
if (!stock.ok) {
  return res.status(409).json({ error: 'out_of_stock' });
}

let intent;
try {
  intent = await stripe.paymentIntents.confirm(order.paymentIntentId, {
    payment_method: req.body.paymentMethodId,
    return_url: 'https://example.com/checkout/complete',
  }, {
    idempotencyKey: `pi-confirm-${order.id}-${attemptNumber}`,
  });
} catch (err) {
  await releaseStock(stock);
  return res.status(402).json({ error: toClientError(err) });
}

return res.json({
  status: intent.status,
  clientSecret: intent.client_secret,
});

Returning the status and client secret to the client is necessary because the result may be requires_action. The browser then has to complete authentication, which it cannot do without the client secret.

// Client, handling the response
const { status, clientSecret } = await response.json();

if (status === 'requires_action') {
  const { error, paymentIntent } = await stripe.handleNextAction({ clientSecret });
  if (error) {
    showMessage(error.message);
  } else if (paymentIntent.status === 'succeeded') {
    showMessage('Payment received.');
  }
} else if (status === 'succeeded') {
  showMessage('Payment received.');
}
Server-side confirmation means you own the authentication round trip. This is the main cost of choosing it. If you take this path and do not handle requires_action, every payment that requires authentication will fail, and in Europe that is a large proportion of them.

Releasing resources on failure

Notice the releaseStock call in the catch block above. If you reserve something before confirming, you must release it on every failure path, including the ones you did not anticipate. A reservation leak is quiet and it compounds.

The subtler problem is the requires_action case. The payment has not failed, but it has not succeeded either, and the customer might abandon it. Reserved stock needs a timeout rather than only an explicit release, because the explicit release will not always run.

Which to choose

Start with client-side confirmation. Move to server-side only when you have a specific thing that must happen between card entry and charge, and when doing it after the charge is genuinely unacceptable. In many cases the cleaner design is to authorise the payment, run your check, and then capture or cancel, which Section 9 describes.

Section 8Authentication and Strong Customer Authentication

Some payments require the customer to prove they are who they say they are, usually through their banking app. Handling this correctly is not optional for European payments, and getting it wrong looks exactly like a high decline rate.

What is actually happening

3D Secure is a protocol that lets a card issuer challenge a transaction before approving it. The customer is shown something from their bank, typically a push notification or a code, and approves the payment there.

In the European Economic Area and the UK, regulation known as Strong Customer Authentication requires this for a large share of online card payments. There are exemptions, for low-value transactions and for some merchant-initiated payments, but you cannot rely on them and you do not control whether they are applied. The issuer decides.

How it appears in the API

When authentication is needed, the PaymentIntent moves to requires_action. The next_action field describes what must happen. In almost all cases you do not need to read that field, because Stripe.js knows how to handle it.

{
  "id": "pi_3Oz...",
  "status": "requires_action",
  "next_action": {
    "type": "use_stripe_sdk"
  },
  "client_secret": "pi_3Oz..._secret_..."
}

With client-side confirmation, Stripe.js presents the challenge automatically. With server-side confirmation you call stripe.handleNextAction() as shown in Section 7.

The failure mode to avoid

The common mistake is code shaped like this:

// Wrong
const intent = await stripe.paymentIntents.confirm(id, { payment_method: pm });
if (intent.status === 'succeeded') {
  await fulfil(order);
} else {
  await markPaymentFailed(order);   // treats requires_action as failure
}

This integration will work in testing with a card that does not trigger authentication, then fail for a large share of real European customers. The payment has not failed. It is waiting for the customer, and the customer never gets the chance because your code has already given up.

The correct shape handles each status explicitly, as in Section 4.

Testing authentication properly

Stripe provides test cards that force an authentication challenge, and cards where the authentication itself fails. Both paths need testing. A card that always requires authentication and then succeeds is not sufficient coverage, because a customer who dismisses the bank's prompt produces a different outcome that your code must also handle.

Scenario to testExpected handling
Authentication required, customer completes itStatus moves to succeeded. Fulfilment proceeds, driven by the webhook.
Authentication required, customer abandons itStatus stays requires_action. No fulfilment. The intent remains usable if they come back.
Authentication required, customer fails itStatus returns to requires_payment_method. Offer another attempt.
Authentication not requiredStraight to succeeded or processing.

Reducing how often authentication is required

You have limited influence, but not none. Sending richer data with the payment, such as billing address and email, allows the issuer to make a better risk assessment and may let it waive the challenge. Stripe passes this through where it can.

Do not try to engineer your way around authentication. Integrations that attempt to avoid it tend to produce higher decline rates, not lower, because an issuer that cannot authenticate a risky-looking payment will often simply decline it.

Section 9Authorise now, capture later

Card payments have two steps. Authorisation reserves the funds against the customer's credit limit. Capture takes them. Most integrations do both at once, but separating them is the right design for several common business models.

When you want this

  • You ship physical goods. Many card networks and consumer protection rules expect you to charge at dispatch rather than at order. Authorising at checkout and capturing at dispatch matches that.
  • The final amount is not known at order time. Hotels, car hire, fuel and anything with a variable final total. You authorise an estimate and capture the actual, which may be lower.
  • You need a human to approve. High-value orders, bespoke manufacturing, anything with a manual fraud review step.
  • Fulfilment can fail after payment. An authorisation that you simply never capture is much cleaner than a charge you have to refund.

How to do it

const intent = await stripe.paymentIntents.create({
  amount: 15000,
  currency: 'gbp',
  capture_method: 'manual',
  automatic_payment_methods: { enabled: true },
  metadata: { order_id: order.id },
});

Once confirmed and authenticated, the intent reaches requires_capture rather than succeeded. The funds are held but not taken.

// Later, when you dispatch
const captured = await stripe.paymentIntents.capture(intent.id, {
  amount_to_capture: 14200,   // optional; defaults to the full amount
}, {
  idempotencyKey: `pi-capture-${order.id}`,
});

Capturing less than the authorised amount is allowed. The difference is released back to the customer's available credit. Capturing more is not allowed, so authorise for the maximum plausible total rather than the expected one.

The constraint that catches people out

Authorisations expire. An uncaptured authorisation is typically valid for about seven days, and the exact window depends on the card network and the issuer. After it expires the hold is released and the money is no longer reserved for you. If your fulfilment can take longer than a week, manual capture alone will not work and you need a different design.

Build an explicit timer. Track authorisation age, alert before expiry, and decide deliberately whether to capture, cancel, or re-authorise. Discovering expired authorisations during month-end reconciliation is a bad way to find out.

Cancelling instead of capturing

If you decide not to fulfil, cancel rather than capturing and refunding.

await stripe.paymentIntents.cancel(intent.id, {
  cancellation_reason: 'abandoned',
});

Valid reasons are duplicate, fraudulent, requested_by_customer and abandoned. Record one that is true, because this field is visible during disputes and in your own analysis of why orders fail.

Cancelling releases the hold. It does not produce a refund on the customer's statement, which is better for them. A capture followed by a refund shows two entries, confuses customers, and in some cases costs you fees.

What the customer sees

An authorisation usually appears as a pending transaction in their banking app. It looks like a charge to most people. Two consequences follow. First, say clearly at checkout that they will be charged at dispatch, or you will field support messages about being charged early. Second, when you capture a lower amount, the pending entry is replaced by the real one, which can look like two transactions briefly. Mention it in your confirmation email if your amounts commonly change.

Section 10Idempotency

Networks fail in the middle of requests. When that happens you do not know whether the operation completed. Idempotency is the mechanism that lets you retry safely without charging a customer twice, and it is the single most important reliability feature in the API.

The problem

Your server sends a request to create and confirm a payment. The connection drops before the response arrives. The payment may have succeeded, or it may never have reached Stripe. From your side the two are indistinguishable.

Without idempotency you have two bad options. Retry, and risk charging twice. Do not retry, and risk a customer who was charged for an order you never created. Both generate support contacts and both erode trust.

How it works

Send an Idempotency-Key header with any POST request. If Stripe has already processed a request with that key, it returns the original response instead of performing the operation again.

curl https://api.stripe.com/v1/payment_intents \
  -u "sk_test_...:" \
  -H "Idempotency-Key: pi-create-order-10482" \
  -d amount=2000 \
  -d currency=gbp

In the SDKs it is an option on the request rather than a body parameter.

await stripe.paymentIntents.create(params, {
  idempotencyKey: `pi-create-${order.id}`,
});

Choosing keys

Keys are strings of up to 255 characters. Stripe suggests V4 UUIDs or another random value with enough entropy to avoid collisions. That advice is correct for the general case, but for retry safety you need something more specific.

A randomly generated key created at the moment of the call does not protect you, because the retry generates a different key and performs the operation again. The key must be derived from the operation, so that the retry produces the same key.

Key strategyProtects against a retry?
uuid() generated at call timeNo. Each attempt is a different key.
`pi-create-${order.id}`Yes, for one creation per order.
`pi-capture-${order.id}`Yes, for one capture per order.
`refund-${order.id}-${refundRequestId}`Yes, and allows multiple distinct refunds.

Generate the key once, store it with the operation, and reuse it on every retry of that same operation. If your framework retries automatically, make sure it preserves the key rather than regenerating one.

Caching behaviour, including the subtleties

For API v1, Stripe caches the result once the operation begins executing, and that includes errors. Retrying a cached 400 with the same key returns the same 400. Stripe also caches 500 responses from POST requests made with an idempotency key, so a retry returns the cached response while it is retained.

Errors that occur before execution begins are not cached. Invalid parameters, an invalid API key and rate limiting all fall into this group. Those requests should be retried after you fix the cause.

Rate limiting runs before the idempotency layer. This means a request rate limited with a 429 can produce a different result later with the same key, because the first attempt never reached the idempotency machinery. Do not assume that a key you have used once is permanently bound to its outcome if the response was a 429.

Reusing a key with different parameters produces an idempotency_error. This is a safety net and it usually indicates a bug in your key derivation. Treat it as an alert rather than something to retry around.

API v2 uses different replay behaviour with a 30-day window. If you are working against v2, read its own idempotency documentation rather than assuming v1 semantics carry over.

What to apply it to

Every POST request that moves money or creates an object. Creation, confirmation, capture, refunds. Do not send idempotency keys on GET requests; they are already safe to repeat and the header has no effect.

A worked example

async function chargeWithRetry(order, params) {
  const key = `pi-create-confirm-${order.id}`;
  const maxAttempts = 3;

  for (let attempt = 1; attempt <= maxAttempts; attempt++) {
    try {
      return await stripe.paymentIntents.create(params, { idempotencyKey: key });
    } catch (err) {
      const retryable =
        err.type === 'api_error' ||
        err.type === 'api_connection_error' ||
        err.statusCode === 429;

      if (!retryable || attempt === maxAttempts) throw err;

      await sleep(Math.min(2 ** attempt * 250, 4000) + Math.random() * 250);
    }
  }
}

The same key is used on every attempt, which is the point. The jitter on the backoff matters when a transient outage causes many of your servers to retry simultaneously.

Section 11Error handling

Stripe returns four types of error. They demand genuinely different responses, and collapsing them into one handler is the most common source of both bad customer experience and undiagnosed bugs.

A handler that distinguishes properly

async function handleStripeCall(fn, context) {
  try {
    return { ok: true, value: await fn() };
  } catch (err) {
    switch (err.type) {
      case 'StripeCardError':
        // Expected. The customer's card was refused.
        logger.info('card_declined', {
          ...context,
          code: err.code,
          declineCode: err.decline_code,
          charge: err.charge,
        });
        return { ok: false, customerMessage: err.message, retryable: false };

      case 'StripeInvalidRequestError':
        // Our bug. Page someone.
        logger.error('stripe_invalid_request', {
          ...context, param: err.param, message: err.message,
        });
        alerting.fire('stripe_invalid_request', context);
        return { ok: false, customerMessage: GENERIC_FAILURE, retryable: false };

      case 'StripeIdempotencyError':
        logger.error('stripe_idempotency_conflict', { ...context });
        alerting.fire('stripe_idempotency_conflict', context);
        return { ok: false, customerMessage: GENERIC_FAILURE, retryable: false };

      case 'StripeRateLimitError':
      case 'StripeAPIError':
      case 'StripeConnectionError':
        logger.warn('stripe_transient', { ...context, type: err.type });
        return { ok: false, customerMessage: TRY_AGAIN, retryable: true };

      case 'StripeAuthenticationError':
        logger.error('stripe_auth_failed', { ...context });
        alerting.fire('stripe_auth_failed', context, { severity: 'critical' });
        return { ok: false, customerMessage: GENERIC_FAILURE, retryable: false };

      default:
        logger.error('stripe_unknown_error', { ...context, err });
        return { ok: false, customerMessage: GENERIC_FAILURE, retryable: false };
    }
  }
}

The important property of this handler is that card declines are logged at info level and never alert anyone, while invalid request errors page someone immediately. If your alerting treats those the same, either you are woken every night by normal declines, or you never find out that a parameter change broke your integration.

The uncertain outcome

A connection error on a confirmation call leaves you genuinely unsure whether the payment went through. Do not guess. Retrieve the PaymentIntent and read its status.

async function resolveUncertainOutcome(paymentIntentId) {
  const intent = await stripe.paymentIntents.retrieve(paymentIntentId);
  return intent.status;
}

This is why creating the PaymentIntent and storing its ID before confirming is valuable. If you have the ID, uncertainty is always resolvable with one read. If you do not, because the connection failed during creation, you need the idempotency key to recover, which is another reason to derive it from your own order ID.

Section 12Declines

Declines are not errors in your system. They are a normal, high-volume outcome, and how you respond to them has a direct and measurable effect on revenue.

Reading a decline

A declined card produces a card_error. Several fields describe what happened, at different levels of precision.

FieldContent
codeA short machine-readable string for the error, such as card_declined.
decline_codeMore specific detail on why the issuer declined, where the issuer provided it.
messageA customer-facing explanation, localised. Show this one.
advice_codeWhere the issuer supplies it, a short string indicating how to proceed.
network_decline_codeThe raw code from the card network, for investigation.
network_advice_codeA two digit code from the network advising the merchant how to proceed.
chargeThe ID of the failed charge, which is useful when a customer disputes that an attempt was made.
doc_urlA link to Stripe's documentation for this error.

The retry decision

Some declines are worth retrying later and some are not. Retrying a hard decline repeatedly can be treated as abusive by card networks, and it will not succeed.

CategoryExample causesAction
Soft, temporaryInsufficient funds, issuer temporarily unavailable, velocity limitsRetry later is reasonable. For subscriptions, schedule a retry over days rather than minutes.
Soft, customer-fixableIncorrect CVC, wrong postcode, expired cardAsk the customer to correct it now. Do not retry the same details.
HardLost or stolen card, pickup card, do not honour with a hard advice code, fraudulentDo not retry. Ask for a different payment method. Do not tell the customer the card was reported stolen.
Blocked by rulesBlocked by your own fraud rulesDo not retry. Review your rules if this is frequent.

Where the issuer provides advice_code or network_advice_code, use it. It is the issuer telling you directly whether another attempt is worth making.

Be careful what you tell the customer about a decline. Stripe's own message is deliberately vague on some decline reasons, and that is intentional. If a card has been reported stolen, telling the person holding it exactly that is a security problem. Show Stripe's message rather than translating the decline code yourself.

Recovering revenue from declines

Three practical measures, roughly in order of value.

Let the customer retry in place. The PaymentIntent returns to requires_payment_method after a decline, so the same intent accepts another attempt. Keep them on the payment step with a clear message and the form ready. Sending them back to the basket loses a meaningful share of them.

Offer an alternative payment method. If a card is declined and you accept other methods, show them. A customer whose card has failed twice is more willing to use a wallet than to find another card.

For saved cards, use network updates. Card networks can tell Stripe about reissued cards, which prevents a predictable wave of declines when cards expire. This matters for recurring billing more than for one-off checkout.

Monitoring

Track decline rate by decline_code, not just in aggregate. Aggregate decline rate is noisy and moves with your traffic mix. A specific code rising sharply is a signal worth acting on. A jump in do_not_honor can indicate a problem with how you are sending data. A jump in fraud-related codes can indicate you are being targeted, or that your own rules are miscalibrated.

Section 13Webhooks

Webhooks are how your system learns what actually happened. Treating them as an optional extra, rather than as the primary source of truth, is the single most consequential design error available in this integration.

Why the browser cannot be your source of truth

After a successful payment the customer is redirected to your return URL and your page reports success. It is tempting to fulfil the order there. Do not.

  • The customer can close the tab during the redirect.
  • Their connection can drop between the bank's authentication page and your site.
  • Some payment methods settle hours or days later, long after the browser session has ended.
  • The redirect can be intercepted or replayed by anyone who wants a free order.

Only your server, having verified a signed event from Stripe, knows that money moved. Everything that must happen exactly once when a payment succeeds belongs in the webhook handler.

Setting up an endpoint

Create an HTTPS endpoint and register it in the Dashboard or via the API, selecting the events you want. Start with a small set and add more as you need them. Subscribing to everything produces volume you will not read.

const endpointSecret = process.env.STRIPE_WEBHOOK_SECRET; // whsec_...

app.post('/webhooks/stripe',
  express.raw({ type: 'application/json' }),
  (req, res) => {
    const sig = req.headers['stripe-signature'];
    let event;

    try {
      event = stripe.webhooks.constructEvent(req.body, sig, endpointSecret);
    } catch (err) {
      logger.warn('webhook_signature_failed', { message: err.message });
      return res.status(400).send(`Webhook Error: ${err.message}`);
    }

    // Acknowledge immediately, process asynchronously.
    enqueue(event);
    res.json({ received: true });
  });
Signature verification needs the raw body. Stripe signs the exact bytes it sent. If your framework parses JSON before your handler runs, verification fails with a message that does not obviously point at the cause. In Express this means express.raw() on this route specifically, mounted before any global JSON body parser. Teams lose hours to this.

Verifying properly

Signature verification is not optional. Without it, anyone who learns your endpoint URL can post a fabricated payment_intent.succeeded event and receive free goods. The Stripe-Signature header and your whsec_ secret are what prevent that.

The SDK handles the comparison correctly, including the timestamp check that prevents replay of an old captured request. Use the SDK's helper rather than implementing HMAC comparison yourself.

Store the signing secret as you would any credential. It is per endpoint, so a test endpoint and a live endpoint have different secrets, and a mismatch presents as every event failing verification.

Acknowledge fast, process later

Return a 2xx quickly. Stripe treats a slow response as a failure and will retry, which means slow processing produces duplicate deliveries on top of whatever made it slow. Push the event onto a queue and process it outside the request.

If your handler does its work inline and that work takes eight seconds, you have built a system that duplicates its own work under load, exactly when it is least able to cope.

Consuming idempotently

Webhook delivery is at least once. You will receive duplicates. Your handler must produce the same result whether an event arrives once or five times.

Every event has a unique id. Record processed IDs and skip repeats.

async function processEvent(event) {
  const inserted = await db.processedEvents.insertIfAbsent({
    id: event.id,
    type: event.type,
    receivedAt: new Date(),
  });

  if (!inserted) {
    logger.info('webhook_duplicate_skipped', { id: event.id });
    return;
  }

  try {
    await route(event);
  } catch (err) {
    // Allow redelivery to retry this event.
    await db.processedEvents.delete(event.id);
    throw err;
  }
}

The insert must be atomic, using a unique constraint on the event ID rather than a read followed by a write. Two deliveries can arrive simultaneously on different workers, and a check-then-act sequence will let both through.

Note the deletion on failure. If processing throws, the record is removed so that Stripe's redelivery can try again. Without that, a transient failure permanently marks the event as handled.

Ordering is not guaranteed

Events can arrive out of order. You can receive payment_intent.succeeded before payment_intent.created. Do not write a handler that assumes a sequence.

The defensive pattern is to treat each event as a trigger to check current state rather than as a state transition in itself. Stripe's own guidance notes that the object included in an event is a snapshot that can be stale, and recommends fetching the latest resource from the API before taking any critical action.

async function onPaymentSucceeded(event) {
  // The embedded object may be stale. Re-read before acting.
  const intent = await stripe.paymentIntents.retrieve(event.data.object.id);

  if (intent.status !== 'succeeded') {
    logger.info('event_superseded', { id: intent.id, status: intent.status });
    return;
  }

  const orderId = intent.metadata.order_id;
  await fulfilOnce(orderId, intent.id);
}

The events worth subscribing to

payment_intent.succeededFulfilling the order. This is the one that matters most.
payment_intent.payment_failedRecording the attempt, notifying the customer if they are not present, releasing reserved stock.
payment_intent.processingShowing a pending state for asynchronous methods. Do not fulfil.

payment_intent.requires_actionUsually nothing automated. Useful for diagnosing drop-off.

Subscribe to refund and dispute events even if you never issue refunds from your own code. Someone will eventually refund from the Dashboard, and without the event your system quietly disagrees with reality.

Section 14Reconciliation

Webhooks are reliable, not infallible. Your endpoint can be down for an hour, a deployment can drop in-flight events, a bug can throw on a subset of payloads. Reconciliation is the safety net that catches what the primary path missed.

Why you need a second mechanism

Consider a deployment that introduces a bug in your webhook handler and is live for forty minutes. Stripe retries failed deliveries, so most of those events will eventually arrive. But if your endpoint returned 200 while failing internally, nothing is retried and those payments are silently unfulfilled.

The customers affected have paid. They will contact you, one at a time, over the following week. Reconciliation turns that into a job that notices in the first hour.

The simplest effective reconciliation

Run a periodic job that looks for payments Stripe considers successful and your system does not.

async function reconcile({ lookbackMinutes = 180 } = {}) {
  const createdAfter = Math.floor(Date.now() / 1000) - lookbackMinutes * 60;
  let startingAfter;
  let checked = 0, repaired = 0;

  do {
    const page = await stripe.paymentIntents.list({
      created: { gte: createdAfter },
      limit: 100,
      starting_after: startingAfter,
    });

    for (const intent of page.data) {
      checked++;
      if (intent.status !== 'succeeded') continue;

      const orderId = intent.metadata.order_id;
      if (!orderId) {
        logger.error('payment_without_order_metadata', { id: intent.id });
        continue;
      }

      const order = await db.orders.find(orderId);
      if (order && order.status === 'paid') continue;

      logger.warn('reconciling_unfulfilled_payment', {
        intentId: intent.id, orderId,
      });
      await fulfilOnce(orderId, intent.id);
      repaired++;
    }

    startingAfter = page.has_more
      ? page.data[page.data.length - 1].id
      : undefined;
  } while (startingAfter);

  metrics.gauge('reconciliation.checked', checked);
  metrics.gauge('reconciliation.repaired', repaired);
}

Run it every fifteen minutes with a lookback window several times longer than the interval, so that overlapping runs cover any gap. It must be safe to run concurrently with webhook processing, which is why fulfilOnce has to be genuinely idempotent.

Reconcile in both directions

The job above finds payments Stripe knows about and you do not. The reverse case matters too: orders your system marked paid where Stripe has no successful payment. That usually indicates a bug in your own code rather than a delivery problem, and it is more serious because you have shipped goods for nothing.

A daily job comparing the two sets, with a report of anything that does not match, is enough. It does not need to repair anything automatically. It needs to tell a person.

What to alert on

SignalWhy it matters
reconciliation.repaired above zeroYour primary path missed something. One is worth investigating. A spike is an incident.
Payments with no order_id in metadataSomething created a payment outside your normal flow, or your metadata is not being set.
Webhook endpoint error rateAvailable in the Dashboard. A rising rate precedes unfulfilled orders.
Orders paid in your system with no matching successful intentPotential revenue loss, and a sign of a logic bug.
Age of oldest unprocessed event in your queueA growing backlog means fulfilment delays that customers will notice before you do.

A note on the metadata dependency

This entire approach depends on metadata.order_id being present on every PaymentIntent. That is why Section 5 recommends setting metadata at creation and treats it as the most useful optional parameter. A payment with no link back to your own records is a manual investigation, and manual investigations do not scale.

Section 15Saving payment methods for later

Charging a returning customer without asking for their card again is straightforward, with one significant complication around authentication that catches most teams the first time.

Saving during a payment

Set setup_future_usage when creating the PaymentIntent. The value tells Stripe how you intend to use the saved method, and that affects how regional authentication requirements are handled.

ValueMeaning
on_sessionFuture charges will happen while the customer is present and able to authenticate.
off_sessionFuture charges will happen when the customer is not present, such as a subscription renewal.
const intent = await stripe.paymentIntents.create({
  amount: 2000,
  currency: 'gbp',
  customer: customer.stripeId,
  setup_future_usage: 'off_session',
  automatic_payment_methods: { enabled: true },
});

Declaring off_session at the time of the first payment allows Stripe to obtain the mandate and authentication needed for later charges, while the customer is still present to complete it. Omitting it and then trying to charge later produces a much higher failure rate, because the authentication that should have happened up front now cannot happen at all.

Get consent explicitly. Saving a payment method requires the customer's agreement, and in several jurisdictions that agreement must be recorded. A checkbox that says you will store the card for future orders, unticked by default, with the text stored alongside the order. This is both a legal requirement in places and useful evidence in a dispute.

Saving without a payment

To collect a card now and charge later, use a SetupIntent rather than a PaymentIntent. It follows the same lifecycle and creates no charge. The statuses are the same set minus the ones that only make sense for a payment.

const setupIntent = await stripe.setupIntents.create({
  customer: customer.stripeId,
  usage: 'off_session',
  automatic_payment_methods: { enabled: true },
});

The client flow mirrors payment confirmation, using stripe.confirmSetup() in place of stripe.confirmPayment().

Charging a saved method

try {
  const intent = await stripe.paymentIntents.create({
    amount: 2000,
    currency: 'gbp',
    customer: customer.stripeId,
    payment_method: savedPaymentMethodId,
    off_session: true,
    confirm: true,
  }, {
    idempotencyKey: `renewal-${subscription.id}-${periodStart}`,
  });

  if (intent.status === 'succeeded') {
    await recordRenewal(subscription, intent);
  }
} catch (err) {
  if (err.code === 'authentication_required') {
    // The issuer wants the customer present. Email them a link.
    await requestCustomerAuthentication(customer, err.raw.payment_intent.id);
  } else {
    await handleRenewalFailure(subscription, err);
  }
}

Two parameters do the work here. off_session: true tells Stripe the customer is not present, and confirm: true attempts the charge immediately rather than requiring a separate call.

The complication

Even with everything set up correctly, an issuer can still demand authentication for an off-session charge. When that happens the request fails with authentication_required, and there is no way to complete it without the customer.

You need a recovery path. The usual shape is an email containing a link back to your site, where the customer completes authentication against the existing PaymentIntent using its client secret. Build this before you launch recurring billing rather than after the first failed renewal cycle, because the alternative is a queue of failed renewals and no mechanism to recover them.

Listing a customer's saved methods

const methods = await stripe.paymentMethods.list({
  customer: customer.stripeId,
  type: 'card',
});

Show the brand, last four digits and expiry when asking a returning customer to confirm which card to use. Showing nothing but "saved card" causes hesitation, especially for customers who have more than one.

Section 16Refunds and cancellation

Cancelling and refunding are different operations with different consequences. Choosing the wrong one costs money and confuses customers.

Which one applies

SituationUseWhy
Authorised but not capturedCancelReleases the hold. No entry on the customer's statement. No fee implications.
Captured, money takenRefundThe only option. Appears as a separate credit on their statement.
Customer changed their mind before dispatch, manual capture in useCancelCleaner for both sides.
Intent is unconfirmed and abandonedCancelTidies your Dashboard. Optional but useful.

Issuing a refund

const refund = await stripe.refunds.create({
  payment_intent: intent.id,
  amount: 500,            // optional; omit for a full refund
  reason: 'requested_by_customer',
  metadata: { refund_request_id: request.id },
}, {
  idempotencyKey: `refund-${request.id}`,
});

Partial refunds are allowed and can be issued repeatedly up to the captured total. The idempotency key is derived from the refund request rather than the order, which allows several distinct refunds against one payment while still protecting each from duplication.

Things worth knowing

  • Refunds take days to appear. Typically five to ten business days depending on the issuer. Say so in your confirmation message, or you will field messages asking where the money is.
  • Fees are generally not returned. A refunded payment usually still costs you the processing fee. For high-refund businesses this is a real line item, and it is an argument for manual capture.
  • Refunds can fail. A closed card account is the usual cause. Subscribe to refund events rather than assuming success.
  • A refund is not a dispute defence. Refunding after a dispute is opened does not automatically close it, and you may pay the dispute fee regardless. Refund early if you are going to.

Cancelling

await stripe.paymentIntents.cancel(intent.id, {
  cancellation_reason: 'requested_by_customer',
});

Cancellation is only valid from certain statuses. A succeeded PaymentIntent cannot be cancelled; it can only be refunded. Attempting to cancel one produces an invalid_request_error, which is a case your code should distinguish rather than treating as a generic failure.

Disputes

A dispute, also called a chargeback, is the customer's bank reversing a payment on their behalf. It is not a refund and you do not control it.

Subscribe to charge.dispute.created and route it to a human immediately. Disputes have evidence deadlines, and missing one means losing by default. Build the alerting even if you expect disputes to be rare, because a dispute that nobody noticed is the worst version of a rare event.

The most effective dispute prevention is not a fraud rule. It is a recognisable statement descriptor, a clear refund policy, and responsive support. A meaningful share of disputes are customers who could not work out what a charge was and could not reach anyone to ask.

The CLI prints a session signing secret. Use it in your local environment rather than the one from the Dashboard, which belongs to a different endpoint.

Automated coverage

Mock Stripe at the HTTP boundary rather than mocking your own wrapper. Mocking your wrapper tests that your wrapper calls your wrapper. Recording real test-mode responses and replaying them gives you fixtures that reflect the actual shape of the API, including the fields you forgot exist.

Keep one small suite that runs against the real test API on a schedule. Mocks drift. A nightly run against test mode catches the day Stripe changes a default or deprecates a parameter, which is considerably better than finding out from a customer.

Load and concurrency

Two concurrency cases are worth a deliberate test. First, the same webhook event delivered to two workers simultaneously, which checks that your deduplication uses a real unique constraint. Second, a reconciliation run executing while webhooks are being processed, which checks that fulfilOnce is genuinely safe under concurrency rather than safe in the usual ordering.

Section 18Going live

A checklist, in the order that the items tend to cause problems.

Credentials

  • Live keys are in a secrets manager or environment variables, never in the repository.
  • Use a restricted API key with only the permissions your code needs, rather than the unrestricted secret key.
  • Key rotation has been tested at least once, so you know the procedure before you need it urgently.
  • Test and live webhook endpoints have separate signing secrets, and the right one is deployed to each environment.
  • No key appears in logs. Check your request logging on payment routes specifically.

Webhooks

  • The live endpoint is registered and reachable over HTTPS with a valid certificate.
  • Signature verification is enforced, and a request with a bad signature is rejected with a 400.
  • The handler acknowledges within a second or two and processes asynchronously.
  • Deduplication is backed by a unique constraint on event ID.
  • You are subscribed to refund and dispute events, not only payment events.
  • Failures alert someone. A silently failing webhook handler is the quietest way to lose orders.

Reconciliation and monitoring

  • The reconciliation job runs on a schedule and its repair count is a monitored metric.
  • Alerts exist for repaired payments, webhook error rate, queue age and unmatched orders.
  • Decline rate is tracked by decline code, not only in aggregate.
  • Someone owns the dispute alert and knows what to do when it fires.

Customer-facing details

  • Statement descriptor is recognisable. Test by making a real small payment and looking at your own statement.
  • Error messages distinguish clearly between "your card was declined, try another" and "something went wrong on our side, you have not been charged".
  • Refund timescales are stated where customers will see them.
  • If you save cards, consent is explicit and recorded.
  • The payment step allows retry in place after a decline.

Operational readiness

  • A runbook exists for the three most likely incidents: webhook endpoint down, elevated decline rate, and a payment succeeded with no order created.
  • Support staff can find a payment from an order reference and vice versa, which is what the metadata is for.
  • Someone other than the person who built it has been walked through the flow.

The first week

Watch the reconciliation repair count daily rather than relying on the alert. In the first week it is the measure most likely to reveal a gap between your test assumptions and production reality. A repair count that is consistently zero is the signal that your primary path is sound.

Section 19A reference implementation

Everything above, assembled. This section walks through a complete server-side implementation with the reasoning attached to each decision, because the individual pieces are simpler than the job of fitting them together.

The shape of it

Five components, each with one responsibility.

ComponentResponsibility
Payment serviceCreates and updates PaymentIntents. The only place that calls Stripe for payment creation.
Webhook receiverVerifies signatures, enqueues events, returns 200 fast. No business logic.

The separation matters. A webhook receiver that fulfils orders inline is the most common design in small integrations and the hardest one to make reliable, because it couples delivery latency to business logic duration.

Payment service

class PaymentService {
  constructor(stripe, db, logger) {
    this.stripe = stripe;
    this.db = db;
    this.logger = logger;
  }

  async intentForOrder(order) {
    if (order.paymentIntentId) {
      const existing = await this.reuseIfPossible(order);
      if (existing) return existing;
    }
    return this.createFor(order);
  }

  async reuseIfPossible(order) {
    let intent;
    try {
      intent = await this.stripe.paymentIntents.retrieve(order.paymentIntentId);
    } catch (err) {
      this.logger.warn('intent_retrieve_failed', {
        orderId: order.id, err: err.message,
      });
      return null;
    }

    const reusable = [
      'requires_payment_method',
      'requires_confirmation',
      'requires_action',
    ];
    if (!reusable.includes(intent.status)) return null;

    if (intent.amount !== order.amountMinor ||
        intent.currency !== order.currency.toLowerCase()) {
      intent = await this.stripe.paymentIntents.update(intent.id, {
        amount: order.amountMinor,
        currency: order.currency.toLowerCase(),
      });
    }
    return intent;
  }

  async createFor(order) {
    const key = order.idempotencyKey ?? `pi-create-${order.id}`;

    const intent = await this.stripe.paymentIntents.create({
      amount: order.amountMinor,
      currency: order.currency.toLowerCase(),
      customer: order.stripeCustomerId ?? undefined,
      description: `Order ${order.reference}`,
      statement_descriptor_suffix: STATEMENT_SUFFIX,
      automatic_payment_methods: { enabled: true },
      metadata: {
        order_id: String(order.id),
        order_reference: order.reference,
        environment: process.env.APP_ENV,
      },
    }, { idempotencyKey: key });

    await this.db.orders.update(order.id, {
      paymentIntentId: intent.id,
      idempotencyKey: key,
    });

    return intent;
  }
}

Two details worth noting. The idempotency key is persisted on the order, so a retry after a crash uses the same key rather than generating a new one. And reuseIfPossible returns null rather than throwing when retrieval fails, so a transient Stripe problem produces a new intent rather than a failed checkout.

Webhook receiver

app.post('/webhooks/stripe',
  express.raw({ type: 'application/json' }),
  async (req, res) => {
    const signature = req.headers['stripe-signature'];
    let event;

    try {
      event = stripe.webhooks.constructEvent(
        req.body, signature, process.env.STRIPE_WEBHOOK_SECRET,
      );
    } catch (err) {
      logger.warn('webhook_bad_signature', { message: err.message });
      return res.status(400).send('Invalid signature');
    }

    try {
      await queue.publish('stripe-events', {
        id: event.id, type: event.type, objectId: event.data.object.id,
      });
    } catch (err) {
      // Could not enqueue. Fail so Stripe retries rather than
      // acknowledging an event we have not stored anywhere.
      logger.error('webhook_enqueue_failed', { id: event.id });
      return res.status(500).send('Could not enqueue');
    }

    return res.json({ received: true });
  });
Note what is published to the queue. The event ID, type and object ID, not the full payload. The processor re-reads the object from the API anyway, because the embedded snapshot can be stale. Keeping the queue message small also keeps it well inside any message size limit.

Event processor

const handlers = {
  'payment_intent.succeeded': onSucceeded,
  'payment_intent.payment_failed': onFailed,
  'payment_intent.processing': onProcessing,
  'payment_intent.canceled': onCanceled,
  'charge.refunded': onRefunded,
  'charge.dispute.created': onDisputeOpened,
};

async function processEvent(message) {
  const claimed = await db.processedEvents.insertIfAbsent({
    id: message.id, type: message.type, receivedAt: new Date(),
  });

  if (!claimed) {
    logger.info('event_duplicate', { id: message.id });
    return;
  }

  const handler = handlers[message.type];
  if (!handler) {
    logger.info('event_unhandled', { type: message.type });
    await db.processedEvents.markProcessed(message.id);
    return;
  }

  try {
    await handler(message);
    await db.processedEvents.markProcessed(message.id);
  } catch (err) {
    // Release the claim so redelivery can retry.
    await db.processedEvents.delete(message.id);
    throw err;
  }
}

The unhandled case is marked processed rather than left open. Events you have deliberately not subscribed to should not accumulate as apparent failures.

Handlers

async function onSucceeded({ objectId }) {
  // Re-read. The event snapshot may be stale or out of order.
  const intent = await stripe.paymentIntents.retrieve(objectId);
  if (intent.status !== 'succeeded') {
    logger.info('succeeded_event_superseded', {
      id: intent.id, status: intent.status,
    });
    return;
  }

  const orderId = intent.metadata.order_id;
  if (!orderId) {
    logger.error('payment_without_order_id', { id: intent.id });
    alerting.fire('payment_without_order_id', { intentId: intent.id });
    return;
  }

  await recordAttempt(orderId, intent, null);
  await fulfilment.fulfilOnce(orderId, intent.id);
}

async function onFailed({ objectId }) {
  const intent = await stripe.paymentIntents.retrieve(objectId);
  const error = intent.last_payment_error ?? {};

  await recordAttempt(intent.metadata.order_id, intent, error);
  await reservations.releaseFor(intent.metadata.order_id);

  if (intent.metadata.off_session === 'true') {
    await notifyCustomerOfFailedPayment(intent);
  }
}

Fulfilment, made idempotent

async function fulfilOnce(orderId, paymentIntentId) {
  // A conditional update is the lock. Only one caller wins.
  const claimed = await db.orders.updateWhere(
    { id: orderId, status: 'pending' },
    { status: 'paid', paymentIntentId, paidAt: new Date() },
  );

  if (!claimed) {
    const order = await db.orders.find(orderId);
    if (order.paymentIntentId && order.paymentIntentId !== paymentIntentId) {
      // Two different payments against one order. Needs a human.
      alerting.fire('order_paid_twice', {
        orderId, existing: order.paymentIntentId, incoming: paymentIntentId,
      });
    }
    logger.info('fulfilment_already_done', { orderId });
    return;
  }

  await dispatchToWarehouse(orderId);
  await sendConfirmationEmail(orderId);
  await db.orders.update(orderId, { fulfilledAt: new Date() });
}

The conditional update is the important line. It uses the database to decide who wins, which works correctly when the webhook processor and the reconciler run at the same moment on different machines. A read followed by a write does not.

The order_paid_twice alert catches a genuine and expensive failure: two successful payments for one order. It should never fire. If it does, somebody needs to refund one of them, and you want to know within minutes rather than at month end.

Section 20Troubleshooting

Symptoms you will actually encounter, with the causes in rough order of likelihood.

Webhook signature verification keeps failing

  1. The body is being parsed before your handler sees it. This is the cause roughly nine times in ten. Stripe signs the raw bytes. A global JSON body parser mounted before your webhook route destroys them. Mount a raw body parser on the webhook route specifically, and make sure route ordering puts it first.
  2. Wrong signing secret. The Dashboard endpoint secret, the CLI session secret and a second environment's secret are all different. They all look like whsec_....
  3. A proxy is rewriting the body. Some gateways normalise JSON or re-encode character sets. Check what actually arrives at your process, not what you sent.
  4. Clock skew. Verification includes a timestamp tolerance. A server whose clock is far out will reject valid requests.

Payments succeed but orders are not created

  1. Your handler returns 200 while failing internally. Stripe sees success and never retries. Check whether your handler catches exceptions and swallows them. This is why reconciliation exists.
  2. Metadata is missing. If order_id is absent, the handler has nothing to act on. Check that every creation path sets it, including any admin tools or scripts.
  3. You are fulfilling from the browser return page. Customers who close the tab never trigger it.
  4. The event type is not subscribed. Easy to miss after adding a new flow that produces a different event.

Some customers always fail at the payment step

  1. requires_action is being treated as a failure. This affects European customers disproportionately and can look like a regional outage. Section 8 covers it.
  2. Your return URL is wrong in one environment. A staging URL in production configuration breaks the redirect for every authenticated payment.
  3. Content security policy is blocking the 3D Secure iframe. The browser console will say so. Easy to introduce with a CSP tightening that nobody connected to payments.

Duplicate charges

  1. Idempotency keys generated at call time. A random key per attempt provides no protection. The key must be derived from the operation.
  2. A new PaymentIntent created per attempt. If a decline causes your code to create a fresh intent and the customer's second card succeeds on the first intent too, you have two payments. Reuse the intent.
  3. Fulfilment not guarded. Two deliveries of one event, both fulfilling, is not a duplicate charge but it is a duplicate dispatch, which costs the same.

The amount charged does not match the order

  1. The basket changed after the intent was created. Update the intent, or recreate it, before confirmation.
  2. Minor unit conversion applied twice, or not at all. A charge of exactly 100 times too much or too little is diagnostic.
  3. Zero-decimal currency treated as decimal. JPY amounts divided by 100 somewhere in your code.

Everything works in test and fails in live

  1. Live keys not deployed, or a test key in live configuration. A test key against live endpoints fails authentication.
  2. Payment methods not enabled in live mode. Dashboard settings are per mode.
  3. The live webhook endpoint was never registered, or points at a URL that is not reachable from outside your network.
  4. Account verification incomplete. Some capabilities are restricted until Stripe has verified the business.

How to investigate any of these

Start with the Dashboard event log and the PaymentIntent itself, not with your own logs. The PaymentIntent records every attempt, the status at each point and the error on each failure. That is the authoritative account of what happened, and comparing it with your logs tells you where your system's view diverged.

Section 21Multiple currencies

Accepting more than one currency introduces problems that have nothing to do with the payment API and everything to do with arithmetic, settlement and presentation.

Decide what the price actually is

There are two models and they behave differently.

ModelHow it worksTrade-off
Fixed prices per currencyYou set £20, €24 and $25 independently.Predictable for customers and for your accounting. Requires maintenance when rates move.
Converted at checkoutOne base price, converted at a rate you fetch.Always current. Produces odd-looking prices, and you carry the rate risk between quoting and settling.

Fixed prices are usually the better choice for anything customer-facing. People notice when a price changes by forty pence between visits, and rounded prices convert better.

Zero-decimal currencies

Most currencies divide into 100 minor units. Several do not. JPY and KRW are the common examples. An amount of 1000 in JPY is one thousand yen.

Never hardcode a divisor of 100. Use a currency table with the exponent, and route every conversion through one function.

const EXPONENT = { JPY: 0, KRW: 0, BHD: 3, KWD: 3, /* default 2 */ };

function toMinorUnits(amount, currency) {
  const exp = EXPONENT[currency] ?? 2;
  return Math.round(amount * 10 ** exp);
}

function toDisplay(minor, currency) {
  const exp = EXPONENT[currency] ?? 2;
  return (minor / 10 ** exp).toFixed(exp);
}

Three-decimal currencies such as KWD and BHD exist and have their own handling rules at the network level. If you plan to accept them, read the specific guidance rather than assuming the general pattern holds.

Settlement

Charging in a currency you do not hold a balance in means a conversion happens somewhere, and it costs something. Where that conversion occurs, and who bears the spread, depends on your Stripe account configuration and the currencies involved.

Two practical consequences. First, your revenue in your reporting currency is not the sum of your charge amounts, so build reporting against the balance transactions rather than the charges. Second, refunds in a converted currency may not return exactly what the customer paid, because the rate has moved. Expect occasional support contacts about small discrepancies and have an answer ready.

Presentation

  • Show the currency explicitly. A bare "$25" is ambiguous across at least a dozen currencies.
  • Format according to the customer's locale, not yours. Decimal separators differ.
  • If you convert, say so and show the rate. Customers who feel a rate was hidden dispute more often.
  • State the charging currency before the payment step, not after. A customer who expected to pay in euros and sees dollars will abandon.

Section 22Monitoring payments in production

Payment systems fail quietly. A broken checkout produces no errors in your application logs if the failure is a decline, and no alert at all if the failure is a webhook that returns 200 while doing nothing.

The four metrics that matter

Payment success rateThe headline number. Attempts that reach succeeded divided by attempts confirmed.Drops more than 5 percentage points against the same hour last week
Reconciliation repair countDirect measure of how often your primary path misses a payment.Above zero, for a first look. Above three in an hour, page someone.
Webhook processing lagAge of the oldest unprocessed event.Above five minutes

Why aggregate success rate is not enough

A single success rate hides the failures that matter. A 3% drop could be one issuer having a bad day, which needs no action, or your authentication handling breaking for one card brand, which needs an immediate rollback.

Break the number down by at least three dimensions: card brand, issuing country, and whether authentication was required. A failure concentrated in one slice is a different problem from a uniform drop, and the slice usually names the cause.

Logging that is useful later

Log one structured line per payment attempt with a consistent shape.

{
  "event": "payment_attempt",
  "order_id": "10482",
  "payment_intent_id": "pi_3Oz...",
  "status": "requires_action",
  "amount_minor": 2000,
  "currency": "gbp",
  "card_brand": "visa",
  "card_country": "GB",
  "authentication_required": true,
  "decline_code": null,
  "attempt_number": 1,
  "latency_ms": 840
}

This one line answers most questions you will later want to ask without needing to reconstruct anything. Note what is absent: no card number, no client secret, no full name, no email. Payment logs end up in systems with broader access than your database, and they are retained for a long time.

Never log the client secret or any part of a card number. It is easy to do accidentally by logging a whole request or response object. Add a redaction layer at the logger rather than relying on every call site to remember.

A dashboard worth having

Six panels, in this order, is usually enough.

  1. Successful payments per hour, with last week overlaid.
  2. Success rate, split by authentication required or not.
  3. Top ten decline codes over the last 24 hours.
  4. Reconciliation repairs, as a bar per run.
  5. Webhook queue depth and oldest message age.
  6. Payment to fulfilment latency, p50 and p95.

Put it where someone sees it daily rather than only during an incident. Most payment problems are visible on this dashboard for hours before a customer reports them.

What to rehearse

Two drills are worth running before you need them. First, disable your webhook endpoint for ten minutes in a staging environment and confirm that reconciliation recovers every payment without manual intervention. Second, walk a support engineer through finding a specific payment from nothing but a customer's email address and an approximate date. If either exercise takes longer than you expected, fix that before launch rather than during an incident.
